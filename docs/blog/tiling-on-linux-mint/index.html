Before you install anything

Tiling on Linux Mint.

Cinnamon comes with more window management than most people ever find: halves, quarters, edge snapping, four workspaces. What it does not come with is a layout that stays. Here is the full inventory, and the three ways to go further, with what each one costs.

greenTile closes that gap: a layout per workspace that windows join and leave by themselves, while the built-in keys and the rest of the desktop stay as they are.

What you already have Where it stops

Everything the keyboard already does.

These are the defaults in Cinnamon 6.x, read from Cinnamon's own keybinding schemas. All of them can be changed under System Settings → Keyboard → Shortcuts → Windows.

  • HalvesSuper+← left half, Super+→ right half, Super+↑ top half, Super+↓ bottom half.
  • QuartersCombine two of them: push left, then push up, and the window sits in the upper left quarter. Push in the opposite direction to undo it.
  • MaximiseAlt+F10.
  • Switch workspaceCtrlAlt+←/→.
  • Take the window alongCtrlAltShift+←/→ moves the focused window to the workspace next door.
  • Workspace overviewExpo (all workspaces as a grid) and Scale (all windows of the current one) are already there. The hot corners can open them; they are simply switched off until you turn a corner on.

The quarter trick deserves one more sentence, because Cinnamon never advertises it. Halves and quarters are the same mechanism: a window remembers the last edge it was pushed against, and pushing it against a perpendicular edge divides the space it already occupies. That is why the second push snaps into place instead of jumping somewhere new.

It also means the space you can address is fixed: a half, or a quarter of a whole screen. There is no way to ask for "one third", except by resizing a half with the mouse, which is exactly the moment the arrangement stops being something the keyboard can reproduce.

Everything the mouse already does.

The oldest trick on the desktop, and still the fastest for a single window.

  • Drag to a side edgeThe window covers that half of the screen, full height. Works on every monitor.
  • Drag to the top edgeThe window maximises.
  • Drag to another monitorIt stays where you drop it. Nothing else moves.
  • Double-click the title barMaximise.

Notice the pattern: every gesture moves one window, and the rest of the screen does not react. Neighbours do not make room, and closing a window leaves a hole. That is not a flaw to fix; it is simply a different promise than the one tiling makes. Here, you are the one who keeps the screen tidy; nothing else is watching it.

The exception proves it: edge tiling only triggers while you are actively dragging. Once you let go, the window is a normal window again: resize it and it stays that size, move it and it takes its edge with it. There is no state that says "this window is in a slot now", because there are no slots.

Where it stops.

None of this is a bug; Cinnamon's window management simply answers a narrower question than tiling does. What is missing, exactly:

  • No layout that exists on its own. There is nothing to save, name or assign. How the windows sit is the result of what you did to each one, and it is gone the moment you rearrange them.
  • Nothing happens by itself. A window that opens gets a size and a place chosen by the application. A window that closes leaves its space empty.
  • One arrangement for all workspaces. Four workspaces are four places to put windows, not four different layouts. Switch over and you start arranging again.
  • No gaps, no rules. Windows share their edges exactly, and there is no way to say "keep this app out".
  • Two at a time is the ceiling. Two halves or four quarters. A third window always covers one of the others unless you resize it by hand, and then the next window that opens breaks the arrangement again.
  • The keyboard cannot cross monitorsHalves are defined per screen, and there is no key that sends a window to the neighbouring monitor. You drag it over with the mouse, then push it into a half on the other side.

Read the list as one sentence: Cinnamon gives you moves, and tiling wants a structure. Every built-in gesture is complete in itself and forgets immediately; a layout is something that persists, that other windows react to, and that survives the next window opening.

An ordinary Tuesday.

Abstract lists are easy to nod at. So here is a concrete morning, with the two windows the built-ins allow and the four applications that actually want space.

Nine twenty. The browser goes left, the editor right: three seconds, both halves filled, and this part works well. Then the terminal comes up, and there is nowhere for it: it opens in the middle, on top of both, at whatever size the application prefers. You push it into the left quarter, which pushes the browser out of that corner and onto the terminal. You resize by hand until the browser is back in view.

Nine forty. You switch to the workspace with the mail client on it. It looks exactly like the workspace you left, has nothing in common with what you arranged there, and you arrange it again: the same four windows, in the same two slots, by hand, for the same reasons as yesterday.

That is the honest case against the built-ins, and it is not about how many keys you can press. It is that the arrangement has no existence of its own: it lives in what you did five minutes ago, and it evaporates on the next window, the next workspace, the next login. Tiling exists to move that arrangement out of your fingers and into something that stays put.

Three ways forward.

All three are legitimate. They differ in what you keep and what you give up.

Costs nothing

Stay with the built-ins

Two windows per screen, halves and quarters, workspaces by hand. Perfectly fine if your work fits in two windows, and the gestures above are already muscle memory for a reason.

Re-read the list
Keeps Cinnamon

Install a tiling extension

You keep the panel, the menu, the themes and the applets, and get layouts on top: presets, auto placement, gaps, rules. This is what greenTile does. The other option is gTile from the Spices catalogue, which places single windows on a grid.

Compare the extensions →
Changes the session

Use a tiling window manager

i3, sway, bspwm and friends tile by nature and are loved for it. The cost is real: you log into a different session, and Cinnamon's panel, applets and menu do not come with it. Choose it at the login screen, not instead of Cinnamon.

i3 on GitHub

The middle road is the one that surprises people, because it does not force the choice. With greenTile installed, Cinnamon is still Cinnamon: the panel, the menu, the applets and the settings are untouched, and the built-in keys from the list above keep working on top of the layout. What changes is only what happens after you stop arranging: windows that open join the arrangement, windows that close leave their space to the neighbours, and each workspace keeps its own shape.

What the middle road gains you.

Four things the built-ins cannot do, and greenTile can.

A layout, not moves

The arrangement exists on its own

You paint the layout once on a grid (columns, their widths, the stacks inside them) and assign it to a monitor and a workspace. From then on it is a thing that persists, not the result of what you did five minutes ago.

Nothing by hand

Windows join and leave by themselves

With auto mode on for a workspace, a window that opens is fitted into the layout a moment later, and when one closes the neighbours grow into the space. No hole to resize shut, no window to steer into place.

Per workspace

Four desks, four shapes

The settings are kept per monitor and per workspace, so “writing”, “browsing” and “chat” each get their own arrangement, and each one is waiting after a switch, a replug or a Cinnamon restart.

Still Cinnamon

You keep the desktop you have

The panel, the menu, the applets, the themes and every built-in key stay exactly as they are. That is the difference from the third option above: no new session, no new habits to relearn.

If you take the middle road.

The install is a zip and a script, and it does not touch the rest of your desktop:

  • Download the latest release, unpack it, run ./install.sh, restart Cinnamon (CtrlAltEsc), then enable greenTile in the Extensions manager.
  • Press SuperCtrlA on a workspace: its windows tile, and from then on new ones join the layout by themselves.
  • Nothing is replaced quietly. The built-in keys above keep working, and greenTile can be switched off at any time; the windows simply stay where they are.
