Before you install anything

Tiling on Linux Mint.

Cinnamon comes with more window management than most people ever find: halves, quarters, edge snapping, four workspaces. What it does not come with is a layout that stays. Here is the full inventory — and the three ways to go further, with what each one costs.

What you already have Where it stops

Everything the keyboard already does.

These are the defaults in Cinnamon 6.x, read from Cinnamon's own keybinding schemas. All of them can be changed under System Settings → Keyboard → Shortcuts → Windows.

  • HalvesSuper+← left half, Super+→ right half, Super+↑ top half, Super+↓ bottom half.
  • QuartersCombine two of them: push left, then push up, and the window sits in the upper left quarter. Push in the opposite direction to undo it.
  • MaximiseAlt+F10.
  • Switch workspaceCtrlAlt+←/→.
  • Take the window alongCtrlAltShift+←/→ moves the focused window to the workspace next door.
  • Workspace overviewExpo (all workspaces as a grid) and Scale (all windows of the current one) are already there. The hot corners can open them — they are simply switched off until you turn a corner on.

Everything the mouse already does.

The oldest trick on the desktop, and still the fastest for a single window.

  • Drag to a side edgeThe window covers that half of the screen, full height. Works on every monitor.
  • Drag to the top edgeThe window maximises.
  • Drag to another monitorIt stays where you drop it. Nothing else moves.
  • Double-click the title barMaximise.

Notice the pattern: every gesture moves one window, and the rest of the screen does not react. Neighbours do not make room, and closing a window leaves a hole.

Where it stops.

None of this is a bug; Cinnamon's window management simply answers a narrower question than tiling does. What is missing, exactly:

  • No layout that exists on its own. There is nothing to save, name or assign. How the windows sit is the result of what you did to each one, and it is gone the moment you rearrange them.
  • Nothing happens by itself. A window that opens gets a size and a place chosen by the application. A window that closes leaves its space empty.
  • One arrangement for all workspaces. Four workspaces are four places to put windows, not four different layouts. Switch over and you start arranging again.
  • No gaps, no rules. Windows share their edges exactly, and there is no way to say "keep this app out".
  • Two at a time is the ceiling. Two halves or four quarters. A third window always covers one of the others unless you resize it by hand — and then the next window that opens breaks the arrangement again.

Three ways forward.

All three are legitimate. They differ in what you keep and what you give up.

Costs nothing

Stay with the built-ins

Two windows per screen, halves and quarters, workspaces by hand. Perfectly fine if your work fits in two windows — and the gestures above are already muscle memory for a reason.

Re-read the list
Keeps Cinnamon

Install a tiling extension

You keep the panel, the menu, the themes and the applets, and get layouts on top: presets, auto placement, gaps, rules. This is what greenTile does, and gTile is the other option in the catalogue.

Changes the session

Use a tiling window manager

i3, sway, bspwm and friends tile by nature and are loved for it. The cost is real: you log into a different session, and Cinnamon's panel, applets and menu do not come with it. Choose it at the login screen, not instead of Cinnamon.

i3 on GitHub

If you take the middle road.

The install is a zip and a script, and it does not touch the rest of your desktop:

  • Download the latest release, unpack it, run ./install.sh, restart Cinnamon (CtrlAltEsc), then enable greenTile in the Extensions manager.
  • Press SuperCtrlA on a workspace: its windows tile, and from then on new ones join the layout by themselves.
  • Nothing is replaced quietly. The built-in keys above keep working, and the extension can be switched off at any time — the windows simply stay where they are.
